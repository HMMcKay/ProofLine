Documentation / Research sources

Case-study record

Citations and claim classifications.

These sources establish that video evidence has been deleted, seized or deliberately disabled. They do not establish that ProofLine would have changed a historical outcome; the “relevance” notes are engineering counterfactuals constrained by the current protocol.

Source method

  • Prefer guilty pleas, court documents, Justice Department materials and official policy records.
  • Label civil allegations and settlements separately from criminal findings.
  • Do not infer facts beyond the cited source.
  • Describe only the failure mode ProofLine addresses: local deletion, device control, missing continuity or deliberate non-recording.
  • Record known limitations when the historical media was received after capture or the camera never started.

Research reviewed August 8, 2026.

Guilty plea

David Quillen — deletion tied to an excessive-force cover-up

Source fact: DOJ reported that Quillen recorded an injured inmate, deleted the video knowing it contained evidence of unlawful force, lied about the deletion and pleaded guilty to conspiracy and destruction of records. DOJ also reported that Samuel Warren admitted the unlawful assault.

Protocol relevance: a capture already streaming through ProofLine could leave durable off-device fragments even if the local video were deleted later.

DOJ, February 22, 2024 (updated February 6, 2025) →

Civil settlement

Khaliah Fitchette — citizen recording seized and deleted

Source fact: the ACLU-NJ settlement release states that an officer seized Fitchette’s phone and deleted her recording. Newark settled and issued training that affirmed recording rights and restricted confiscation, viewing and deletion without a warrant.

Classification: this is a civil settlement source, not a criminal conviction.

Protocol relevance: taking the phone would not erase server-acknowledged fragments.

ACLU-NJ, November 19, 2012 →

Guilty plea

Grayson Sherrill — videos recorded at the Capitol later deleted

Source fact: DOJ reported that Sherrill pleaded guilty to assaulting, resisting or impeding law enforcement and deleted videos from his phone that he took at the Capitol on January 6, 2021.

Protocol relevance: media captured through ProofLine would be committed during recording rather than relying on the later state of the phone’s gallery.

U.S. Attorney’s Office for D.C., February 3, 2023 →

Guilty plea

Abdimajid Nur — juror-bribery video deleted

Source fact: DOJ reported that Nur received a recording of the attempted cash-bribe delivery, deleted it to conceal his involvement, deleted other evidence and pleaded guilty to bribery of a juror.

Protocol limit: Nur received this video after it was recorded. Current ProofLine intentionally accepts only protocol-created capture streams, not arbitrary general uploads. The example demonstrates deletion risk but is not a claim that the current app could have ingested that received file.

U.S. Attorney’s Office for Minnesota, July 23, 2024 →

Guilty plea · boundary case

Nicholas Duty — body camera deliberately disabled or removed

Source fact: DOJ reported that Duty admitted two counts of destroying, altering or falsifying records by purposefully deactivating or removing a body-worn camera to impede a federal investigation.

Protocol limit: ProofLine cannot preserve media from a camera that never begins capture. If a valid chain already exists, subsequent loss of fragments becomes stalled/interrupted rather than an invisible edit.

U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of Ohio, January 22, 2025 →

Federal filing · allegation

Christopher Sharp — recording deletion allegation and federal guidance

Source fact: in a federal court letter, DOJ summarized Sharp’s allegation that Baltimore police officers seized, searched and deleted his phone contents after he recorded an arrest. DOJ argued that policy should prohibit deletion or destruction of recordings under any circumstances.

Classification: the deletion is described as an allegation in a civil case. The source is used for the allegation and DOJ’s policy position, not as a criminal finding.

DOJ Civil Rights Division court letter, May 14, 2012 (PDF) →
