Public video provenance · ProofLine v2

A recording should not disappear with the phone.

ProofLine sends signed video fragments to a durable public evidence service while capture is still happening. If the device is seized, destroyed, stolen, wiped, or disconnected, the server keeps the exact bytes it already acknowledged.

The problem is documented

Recordings have been deleted, seized, destroyed or deliberately disabled.

These examples use government records, guilty pleas, court materials and clearly labeled civil-settlement reporting. ProofLine would not decide whether the depicted conduct was lawful; it addresses the narrower engineering failure that a locally controlled recording can vanish before independent review.

Guilty plea · deletion

Video deleted during an excessive-force cover-up

Eastern Correctional Institution, Maryland · conduct in 2021 · plea announced 2024

The Justice Department reported that correctional officer David Quillen recorded an injured inmate, then deleted the video after officers said it looked bad for the officer who used force. Quillen pleaded guilty to conspiracy and destruction of records; the officer who assaulted the inmate also admitted the unlawful assault.

ProofLine relevance: fragments acknowledged during recording would remain off-device even if the camera or local file were later erased.

Civil settlement · citizen video

Officer seized a teenager’s phone and deleted her recording

Newark, New Jersey · incident in 2010 · settlement announced 2012

According to the ACLU of New Jersey’s settlement announcement, Khaliah Fitchette recorded police activity on a bus. An officer seized her phone and deleted the video. Newark settled the suit and issued training stating that officers could not confiscate, delete or demand to inspect citizen recordings without a warrant.

ProofLine relevance: seizure of the handset would not revoke server receipts or erase fragments already stored remotely.

Baltimore recording-deletion allegations shaped federal guidance

Baltimore, Maryland · incident alleged in 2010 · DOJ guidance in 2012

In a federal filing concerning Christopher Sharp’s civil case, the Justice Department described his allegation that officers seized his phone and deleted recordings after he filmed an arrest. DOJ stated that police policy should prohibit deleting or destroying recordings under any circumstances.

The engineering response

Move the evidence boundary away from the handset.

ProofLine does not try to make a phone indestructible. It narrows the loss window by creating short, signed, independently receipted units while recording is underway.

Sign at the capture edge

An ephemeral session key signs each fragment envelope. The device identity key binds that session to a pseudonymous public fingerprint and explicit assurance level.

Verify before acknowledging

The gateway independently hashes the request body, validates continuity and writes durable object storage before signing a receipt.

Keep original history append-only

Duplicates, conflicts, gaps, recovery supplements and tombstones stay observable. Derived playback and reports never overwrite originals.

Use exact completeness language

A valid signed end can seal declared tracks. An interruption asserts only the highest contiguous prefix the server accepted.

What survives device loss

Every fragment for which the client received—or can later verify—a valid durable server receipt.

What may still be lost

Unacknowledged fragments buffered only on a device that is destroyed before reconnection.

What cryptography cannot prove

That a scene was not staged, that a compromised camera stack was honest, who pressed Stop, or that the phone’s sensors reported objective truth.

The claim boundary

High-assurance provenance—not objective truth.

Can establish

Integrity and received continuity

Whether exported bytes match fragment hashes, signed chains, durable receipts, end manifests and audit history.

Can corroborate

Time, place and device context

Attestation, server time, optional TSA anchors, location, motion, pressure and device-state observations.

Cannot establish

Scene truth or admissibility

Whether the scene was staged, sensors were spoofed, an operator was coerced, or a court will admit or credit the evidence.
